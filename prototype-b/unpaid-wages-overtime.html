New York workers may have claims when employers fail to pay for all hours worked, improperly deny overtime, or make other unlawful wage decisions. The information below explains important New York legal issues; it is not a determination that a particular claim is valid or timely.

When Overtime Pay May Be Required

Many nonexempt employees must receive overtime pay for qualifying hours worked beyond 40 in a workweek. Federal and New York law contain exemptions and industry-specific rules, so a job title alone does not settle eligibility.

Employers may not avoid overtime merely by calling someone a manager or paying a salary; actual duties and applicable tests matter.

Common Wage Violations

Examples can include off-the-clock work, unpaid pre-shift duties, inaccurate time records, failure to pay an applicable overtime rate, and unlawful deductions. A careful review of schedules, pay records and job duties is important.

What Can Be Recovered?

New York Labor Law § 198 and § 663 can provide unpaid compensation and other remedies in qualifying cases. Certain New York wage claims have a six-year limitations period, while federal claims can have different periods.

Documents Worth Saving

  • Pay stubs, wage notices and time sheets
  • Work schedules and employer messages
  • Records of work before or after clocked shifts
  • Job descriptions and actual duties
  • Personal notes identifying hours and pay discrepancies

Frequently Asked Questions

Am I entitled to overtime if I am paid a salary?

Possibly. Salary alone does not establish an exemption. Duties, compensation and the governing law matter.

Can I recover unpaid wages after leaving the job?

Potentially. Leaving employment does not necessarily extinguish an otherwise timely wage claim.
