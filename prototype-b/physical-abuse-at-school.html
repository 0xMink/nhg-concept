When a child is physically harmed at school, a legal review should examine the specific conduct, the people involved, and whether the school failed to meet a legal duty.

Physical Abuse and Excessive Force

Cases may involve intentional physical injury by an employee or volunteer, inappropriate restraint or corporal punishment, or foreseeable harm caused by inadequate supervision. Not every accidental injury is abuse, and the available claims depend on the facts.

Who May Be Responsible?

Depending on the circumstances, an individual may face a claim for intentional misconduct, while a school or district may face a separate negligence claim. When another student is involved, the school's supervision and notice of prior conduct may be especially relevant.

New York Law and Filing Rules

Education Law Article 23-B defines certain forms of child abuse in an educational setting and provides reporting procedures. Other negligence and intentional-tort principles may also apply. Importantly, the special filing rules for childhood sexual abuse do not automatically extend to all physical-abuse claims.

Education Law § 1125

Definitions of child abuse

Defines certain physical and sexual abuse by school employees or volunteers for Article 23-B purposes.

Common Questions

Can a school be responsible if another student caused the injury?

Potentially, if the legal elements of negligent supervision are established. The school is not automatically responsible for every student-on-student incident.

Do the Child Victims Act rules apply to physical abuse?

Not automatically. The special provisions discussed in CPLR § 208(b) concern qualifying childhood sexual-abuse conduct; physical-abuse claims may follow other rules.
