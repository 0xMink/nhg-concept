When a nursing home fails to provide legally required care or protect a resident from foreseeable harm, the resident or an authorized representative may have a claim. The information below explains important New York legal issues; it is not a determination that a particular claim is valid or timely.

Falls and Inadequate Supervision

Nursing home residents may need assistance with walking, transfers or daily activities. 10 NYCRR § 415.12 addresses accident hazards, adequate supervision and assistive devices in covered nursing homes.

A fall does not establish liability by itself. Records showing assessed fall risk, care plans, staffing, supervision and whether an injury was avoidable can be important.

Other Signs of Possible Neglect

Examples may include untreated pressure injuries, malnutrition, dehydration, medication mistakes, delayed medical attention or failure to address abuse allegations. A thorough investigation is necessary to distinguish negligent care from unavoidable complications.

A Special New York Statute: Public Health Law § 2801-d

Section 2801-d provides a private action when a qualifying residential health care facility deprives a patient of a protected right or benefit and that deprivation causes injury, subject to statutory defenses. It can provide a legal theory distinct from ordinary negligence or medical malpractice.

The statute should not be described as automatically applying to every assisted living residence or other care setting.

What Families Can Preserve

  • Care plans, fall-risk assessments and medication records
  • Photographs and dated descriptions of injuries
  • Incident reports, staff communications and visitor observations
  • Facility complaint and inspection history
  • Names of treating clinicians and witnesses

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a family sue after a nursing home fall?

Possibly, if the facts establish a relevant duty, breach or statutory deprivation and resulting injury.

Is a Public Health Law § 2801-d claim the same as malpractice?

No. The statutory cause of action has distinct elements, although multiple theories may arise from the same events.

What if a resident was abused by staff?

The conduct may implicate reporting requirements and civil claims; the appropriate parties and legal theories depend on the facts.
