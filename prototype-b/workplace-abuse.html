When an employer, supervisor, or co-worker engages in sexual misconduct, the legal issues can involve both employment protections and civil claims for intentional harm. NHG has experience representing women abused by employers and understands the importance of a careful, discreet legal review.

When an Employer May Be Legally Responsible

Potential liability depends on who engaged in the conduct, the employer's role, whether it knew or should have known of misconduct in circumstances where that matters, and the particular law being applied. A supervisor's conduct can raise different legal issues than misconduct by a co-worker or third party.

New York's employment discrimination protections may apply alongside separate claims against an individual wrongdoer. Employer liability is not automatic under every legal theory.

Sexual Assault and Unwanted Physical Contact

Sexual assault, unwanted touching, threats, and coercion may give rise to civil claims, and in some cases criminal proceedings. A civil claim can seek compensation for harm, but the available causes of action and deadlines vary according to the conduct.

New York CPLR § 213-c provides a special limitations period for certain specified sexual offenses. It does not apply to every form of harassment or unwanted contact.

Workplace Harassment, Coercion, and Retaliation

New York State Human Rights Law prohibits unlawful harassment based on sex and other protected characteristics, and protects against certain forms of retaliation. Under Executive Law § 296(1)(h), covered harassment need not satisfy the older severe-or-pervasive standard, although petty slights and trivial inconveniences are treated differently.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I sue my employer if a supervisor sexually assaulted me?

Potentially. The answer depends on the alleged misconduct, employment relationship, applicable statutes, and the legal theory supporting employer responsibility.

Is sexual harassment the same as sexual assault?

No. Harassment is an employment-law concept involving prohibited conduct in a work setting; assault can involve separate intentional-tort and criminal-law questions. The same facts can sometimes support more than one type of claim.

What if I was fired after reporting misconduct?

Retaliation protections may apply. Preserve records of the report and subsequent employment actions, and obtain legal advice about deadlines.

Does the law require harassment to be severe or pervasive?

New York State Human Rights Law no longer uses that older threshold for covered harassment claims under Executive Law § 296(1)(h). The statutory standard still has limits and requires analysis.

How much detail do I need to share initially?

You can begin with a brief description. The firm can explain how to arrange a more detailed conversation.
