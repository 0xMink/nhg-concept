Who Can Be Responsible?

A construction accident can involve a general contractor, property owner, subcontractor, equipment provider, or another party. Identifying the legally responsible person or company requires examining who controlled the work, the nature of the hazard, and the specific legal duty involved.

Workers’ compensation often limits direct negligence suits against an employer. It does not necessarily eliminate claims against negligent third parties or statutory claims against other responsible entities.

How New York Labor Laws Protect Workers

Labor Law § 240(1) addresses certain elevation-related risks, including inadequate ladders and scaffolds. Labor Law § 241(6) concerns construction, excavation, and demolition safety requirements and frequently depends on a sufficiently specific Industrial Code rule. Labor Law § 200 concerns general workplace safety duties.

These statutes do not apply identically to every jobsite incident. The work performed and how the injury occurred are important.

Evidence That May Matter

  • Photographs of the worksite and safety equipment
  • Incident reports and witness accounts
  • Contracts identifying owners, contractors and subcontractors
  • Work orders, training records and inspection materials
  • Medical documentation and work restrictions

Compensation and Timing

Potential recovery may include medical expenses, lost earnings, and pain and suffering where legally recoverable. Workers’ compensation liens or other offsets may affect a case. Prompt investigation is especially important because worksites change and evidence can disappear.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I sue if I receive workers’ compensation?

Possibly. Workers’ compensation and a third-party personal injury claim are different. An attorney must review the identity of the defendants and the statutory duties involved.

Does every construction fall qualify under the Scaffold Law?

No. The work, elevation-related risk, protective devices, and causal connection matter.
