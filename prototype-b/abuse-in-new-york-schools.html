When a child experiences sexual or physical abuse in a school setting, families deserve a clear explanation of their legal options. NHG has represented children harmed in schools and understands the importance of investigating both the individual misconduct and whether an institution failed to meet its responsibilities.

When a School May Be Legally Responsible

Depending on the facts, potential defendants may include the individual who committed the abuse, a school or school district, and other responsible parties. A school is not automatically liable for every harmful act. Claims may depend on negligent hiring, retention or supervision, foreseeability, notice of warning signs, and whether the school's conduct contributed to the injury.

Sexual Abuse by Teachers, Coaches, or Staff

Abuse may involve teachers, coaches, administrators, employees, or volunteers. An investigation can examine prior complaints, patterns of inappropriate conduct, failures to respond to reports, hiring records, and the circumstances in which access to the student occurred.

The law distinguishes the individual wrongdoer's responsibility from an institution's potential negligence. A civil claim may involve different legal theories, depending on the evidence.

Physical Abuse and Negligent Supervision

Physical abuse can include excessive or inappropriate force by a staff member. Harm may also occur when a school fails to provide reasonable supervision and an otherwise foreseeable incident takes place. The analysis can differ depending on whether the alleged wrongdoer was a school employee or another student.

What New York Requires Schools to Report

New York Education Law Article 23-B sets out procedures for allegations of child abuse in an educational setting by school employees or volunteers. Sections 1126 and 1128 describe duties for specified school personnel and administrators, including written reporting and, in circumstances described by the statute, referral to law enforcement.

A violation of a reporting requirement does not automatically establish a civil damages claim. The applicable duty, evidence, and available legal remedy must be analyzed separately.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a school be sued for abuse by a teacher?

Potentially. A claim may be brought against the individual and, depending on the facts, against a school or other institution. Whether the institution is liable may depend on its own conduct, notice, foreseeability, and other legal requirements.

Does abuse have to occur on school grounds?

Not necessarily for every possible claim. But where the conduct occurred, whether it was connected to school activities, and what school personnel knew may be crucial.

What if the abuse happened many years ago?

Qualifying childhood sexual-abuse claims may be subject to CPLR § 208(b). The Child Victims Act also created a temporary revival window that has closed. An attorney should evaluate the particular dates, defendants, and claims.

Are the rules the same for physical abuse?

No. The special childhood sexual-abuse statute does not automatically cover every physical-abuse claim. Other limitations and public-entity notice requirements may apply.

Do I need to provide details in my first call?

No. You can begin with a brief description and ask how to arrange a conversation with an attorney. Avoid sending highly sensitive information through unsecured channels.
