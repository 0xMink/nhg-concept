What Is the Scaffold Law?

Labor Law § 240(1) requires covered owners, contractors and their agents to furnish or erect appropriate safety devices for certain work involving buildings and structures. It is commonly called the Scaffold Law, but it can apply to more than traditional scaffolds.

Covered work, the nature of the elevation risk, and the protective device involved all matter. There are statutory exceptions, including certain one- and two-family dwelling owners who do not direct or control the work.

When May Liability Arise?

Examples can involve an unsecured ladder, a failed scaffold, or a falling object that required securing for the work being performed. A violation must be a proximate cause of the injury; an accident alone does not establish liability.

An owner or general contractor may face liability even without direct supervision of the worker in a qualifying case. Whether the worker’s conduct was the sole proximate cause can be a separate issue.

What About Worker Fault?

Comparative negligence generally does not reduce recovery on an otherwise established § 240(1) claim. However, a sole-proximate-cause defense can be significant where adequate safety devices were available and the worker’s actions alone caused the incident.

The facts should be reviewed carefully before drawing conclusions about responsibility.

What If § 240 Does Not Apply?

A worker may have other claims under Labor Law § 241(6), Labor Law § 200, or common-law negligence. These claims have different elements and defenses.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a worker have to fall off a scaffold?

No. Some ladder and falling-object incidents can involve § 240(1), but the particular elevation hazard and causal connection must be established.

Is a property owner automatically liable?

No. The statutory work and risk must be covered, and a violation must cause the injury.
