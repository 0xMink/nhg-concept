After a New York automobile collision, liability may depend on traffic-law violations, witness evidence, vehicle ownership, insurance rules and the nature of the injuries. The information below explains important New York legal issues; it is not a determination that a particular claim is valid or timely.

How Fault Is Determined

Police reports can be helpful, but they do not conclusively determine civil liability. Attorneys examine the collision sequence, photographs, statements, traffic signals, available video and applicable Vehicle and Traffic Law provisions.

Some accidents involve more than one negligent driver. The rules governing comparative fault and recoverable damages can depend on the date and type of claim.

Can the Vehicle Owner Be Responsible?

Vehicle and Traffic Law § 388 can make an owner responsible for injuries caused by the negligence of someone operating the vehicle with the owner’s permission, subject to the statute and applicable defenses.

No-Fault Benefits and Serious Injury

New York’s no-fault system provides specified first-party benefits for covered losses, subject to eligibility and policy terms. A separate lawsuit for pain and suffering may be subject to the statutory serious-injury threshold.

New York amended significant motor-vehicle injury rules in 2026. The effective date, commencement date and precise statutory text should be reviewed rather than relying on older internet summaries.

What to Preserve

  • Vehicle photographs and accident location details
  • Police report and witness information
  • Medical treatment records and expenses
  • Insurance communications and lost-income records

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I sue if the other driver was not ticketed?

Potentially. A traffic citation is not a prerequisite for civil liability.

Can I recover for pain and suffering?

That depends on the statutory threshold, injury evidence, and other rules applicable to the particular claim.
